Blood Pressure: Understanding Your Reading

Blood pressure is the force that circulating blood exerts against the walls of the arteries. The heart generates this pressure with every beat, pushing blood through the cardiovascular system to deliver oxygen and nutrients to organs and tissues. Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) and expressed as two numbers: systolic over diastolic.

Systolic Pressure (the top number) is the pressure in the arteries when the heart contracts and pumps blood out. It represents the peak pressure during a heartbeat.

Diastolic Pressure (the bottom number) is the pressure in the arteries when the heart is at rest between beats, refilling with blood. It represents the minimum pressure in the arterial system.

A reading of 120/80 mmHg is read as "120 over 80."

Standard Classification Table

The following categories are widely used (values in mmHg):

Normal:          Systolic < 120   AND  Diastolic < 80
Elevated:        Systolic 120–129 AND  Diastolic < 80
High BP Stage 1: Systolic 130–139 OR   Diastolic 80–89
High BP Stage 2: Systolic ≥ 140   OR   Diastolic ≥ 90
Hypertensive Crisis: Systolic > 180 AND/OR Diastolic > 120 (requires urgent attention)

Low blood pressure (hypotension) is generally considered a systolic reading below 90 mmHg or diastolic below 60 mmHg, though this is context-dependent and not inherently dangerous for many individuals.

What Affects Blood Pressure Readings

Many factors can temporarily or chronically influence blood pressure:
- Stress and anxiety: The body's stress response releases hormones that raise heart rate and constrict blood vessels, increasing pressure temporarily.
- Physical activity: Exercise temporarily raises blood pressure during exertion; regular aerobic exercise tends to lower resting blood pressure over time.
- Salt (sodium) intake: High sodium causes the body to retain water, increasing blood volume and pressure.
- Hydration: Dehydration reduces blood volume and can lower pressure.
- Body weight: Excess weight increases the workload on the heart and is associated with higher blood pressure.
- Sleep: Poor or insufficient sleep is linked to elevated pressure.
- Caffeine and alcohol: Can cause short-term spikes in blood pressure.
- Medications: Many drugs, including some over-the-counter pain relievers and decongestants, can raise blood pressure.

How Blood Pressure Is Measured

Blood pressure is measured using a sphygmomanometer (a cuff placed around the upper arm) combined with a pressure gauge and sometimes a stethoscope or electronic sensor. For an accurate reading:
- Sit quietly for 5 minutes before measuring.
- Keep your arm at heart level.
- Avoid caffeine, exercise, and smoking for 30 minutes beforehand.
- Take two or three readings and average them.

A single elevated reading does not necessarily indicate hypertension. Blood pressure fluctuates throughout the day. Healthcare providers typically require consistently elevated readings on multiple occasions before diagnosing high blood pressure.

Why Regular Monitoring Matters

Chronic high blood pressure (hypertension) is called a "silent killer" because it usually has no symptoms yet silently damages arteries, the heart, kidneys, and brain over time. It is a major risk factor for stroke, heart attack, heart failure, and kidney disease. Regular monitoring helps detect sustained elevation early, allowing intervention before damage occurs.
