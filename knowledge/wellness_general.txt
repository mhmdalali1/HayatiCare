General Wellness: Sleep, Hydration, Physical Activity, and Stress

Good general health depends on several interconnected lifestyle factors. While individual needs vary and specific advice should always come from a healthcare professional, the following information reflects widely accepted general guidelines.

Sleep

Sleep is a biological necessity, not a luxury. During sleep, the body repairs tissues, consolidates memory, regulates hormones, and supports immune function. Chronic sleep deprivation has been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, mental health conditions, and reduced immune response.

Recommended sleep duration by age group (general guidelines):
- Newborns (0–3 months): 14–17 hours per day
- Infants (4–11 months): 12–15 hours
- Toddlers (1–2 years): 11–14 hours
- School-age children (6–12 years): 9–11 hours
- Teenagers (13–18 years): 8–10 hours
- Adults (18–64 years): 7–9 hours
- Older adults (65+ years): 7–8 hours

General sleep hygiene tips to improve sleep quality:
- Maintain a consistent sleep schedule, even on weekends.
- Keep the bedroom cool, dark, and quiet.
- Limit exposure to screens (phones, tablets, televisions) in the hour before bed, as blue light can suppress melatonin production.
- Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening.
- Avoid large meals close to bedtime.
- Regular physical activity during the day can improve sleep quality, though vigorous exercise very close to bedtime may make it harder to fall asleep.

Hydration

Water is essential for virtually every bodily process: regulating body temperature, transporting nutrients, removing waste products, lubricating joints, and supporting organ function.

General daily water intake guidelines:
- Adult men: approximately 3.7 litres (about 13 cups) of total fluid from all sources per day
- Adult women: approximately 2.7 litres (about 9 cups) of total fluid per day
Note: These figures include water from food (which contributes approximately 20% of fluid intake) as well as beverages.

Signs of dehydration include: dark yellow or amber urine, infrequent urination, dry mouth, headache, fatigue, dizziness, and reduced skin elasticity. Mild dehydration can impair concentration and physical performance.

Factors that increase fluid needs:
- Hot or humid weather
- Physical activity and sweating
- High altitude
- Fever, vomiting, or diarrhoea
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding
- Certain health conditions (check with your doctor)

A practical indicator of adequate hydration is pale yellow urine. Very dark urine typically signals the need to drink more fluids.

Physical Activity

Regular physical activity supports cardiovascular health, weight management, bone density, mental wellbeing, blood sugar regulation, and sleep quality. A sedentary lifestyle is a significant risk factor for many chronic diseases.

General recommendations for adults:
- At least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week (e.g. brisk walking, cycling, swimming), OR
- 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week (e.g. running, fast cycling), OR
- An equivalent combination of both
- Muscle-strengthening activities (e.g. weight training, bodyweight exercises, yoga) on 2 or more days per week
- Older adults should also include balance and flexibility exercises to reduce fall risk

Types of exercise:
- Aerobic (cardio): Raises heart rate; improves cardiovascular fitness (walking, swimming, dancing)
- Strength/resistance: Builds muscle mass and bone density (weights, resistance bands)
- Flexibility: Improves range of motion and reduces injury risk (stretching, yoga)
- Balance: Reduces fall risk, especially important for older adults (tai chi, standing exercises)

Stress

Acute (short-term) stress is a normal adaptive response. However, chronic stress — stress that persists over weeks, months, or years — can have serious effects on health. Prolonged stress elevates cortisol, which can raise blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol; suppress immune function; disrupt sleep; and contribute to anxiety and depression.

General approaches that may help manage stress:
- Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing and slow, deep breaths activate the parasympathetic nervous system and reduce acute stress responses.
- Regular physical activity is one of the most evidence-backed ways to reduce stress and improve mood.
- Adequate sleep is both protective against stress and restored by stress management.
- Social connection and support from friends, family, and community are important buffers against stress.
- Time in nature has been associated with reductions in stress hormones.
- Limiting caffeine and alcohol, which can amplify anxiety symptoms.
